About

Yoon Walker is a historian of modern and contemporary Korea and a Research Associate at the Centre of Korean Studies, SOAS University of London.

Her work centers on the Korean War, human rights under South Korea's authoritarian regimes, and the interpretation of difficult and contested pasts. She completed her PhD in Korean Studies at SOAS in 2026, with a dissertation on institutionalized human rights violations during the authoritarian period between the 1960s and the 1980s.

Alongside her research, she writes and speaks for audiences beyond the university, and has helped build community among scholars of Korea. She currently serves as Secretary of the British Association of Korean Studies, and has been Chair of Korean Researchers in the UK and a council advisor to the UK chapter of the Peaceful Unification Advisory Council.

Research

Her research asks how states build violence into their own institutions, how societies later come to terms with that violence, and who decides which painful pasts are remembered and which are left in silence.

Her doctoral work examined three institutions of confinement and forced labor that operated under South Korea's authoritarian governments — Sŏn'gam Academy, the Sŏsan Development Group, and Brothers Home. Reading them together against the administrative record, she argues that these were not isolated scandals but state violence embedded in official policy, and that all three shared strikingly similar structures, practices, and forms of violence despite operating in different times and places.

Her current project turns from how violence was institutionalized to how its memory is made public, following three sites — Gwanghwamun Square, the May 18th Democratic Plaza in Gwangju, and the Jeju 4·3 Peace Park — where a place of repression became a place of public memory. She is also developing her dissertation into a monograph.

Teaching

Yoon has taught across modern Korean and East Asian history at SOAS, as a lecturer for Contemporary Korean History and as a seminar tutor for The Other Korea: North Korea since 1945, East Asian Imperialisms, and History and Memory in East Asian Cultures.

She believes students learn best when they have room to form their own ideas, exchange them with peers, and receive feedback in a comfortable setting. She teaches with film, music, and current events as much as textbooks, and draws on the diversity of her classrooms as a shared learning resource. She also enjoys mentoring students informally, from those preparing to study in Korea to those applying to graduate programs.
